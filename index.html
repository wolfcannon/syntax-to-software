21 steps and two level-ups

From Syntax to Software

  • One question for the whole course: does neighbourhood walkability predict bike commuting, and what explains the places that don't fit?
  • Every exercise runs in your browser. Nothing to install.
  • Real data, with the failures left in.

For someone with a strong mathematical background and no Python experience, aimed at data work. Each step teaches one layer of the language or of the development process and advances the same project: by step 3 you are building census block group identifiers, by step 7 working out why 12 of them refuse to join.

How it works

  • These pages explain the ideas and check your work. Your answers and your progress stay in this browser.
  • Your own machine is where the project ends up. Two level-ups move it there — into a notebook after Unit 1, then into a project that runs from a terminal. Each arrives once you have something worth putting in it.

About the hidden checks

Each exercise runs your code, then runs a handful of assertions you don't see. In step 18 we stop hiding them: the harness that has been grading you turns out to be a few dozen lines of pytest, and you start writing your own.

Getting started

The pages need serving over HTTP rather than opening off the disk:

git clone <this repo>
cd syntax-to-software
python3 -m http.server 8000
# then open http://localhost:8000

The first exercise you run downloads the Python runtime (Pyodide, about 10 MB) from a CDN, so step 1 needs an internet connection. Nothing else phones home.

The data

Both datasets are in data/. Nothing to download.

SourceWhat it providesRows
EPA National Walkability Index Walkability score 1–20 plus its four ranked inputs 632 block groups
Census ACS 5-year, table B08301 Commuters by mode of transportation 630 block groups
PeopleForBikes City Ratings Bike network quality, 0–100 (capstone only) by city

They do not line up. 7 block groups have a walkability score and no commute data, 5 have commute data and no score, 14 have no workers at all, and 9 carry the census "estimate unavailable" sentinel -666666666. You meet all four in Unit 1.

One honest caveat

The walkability data is real. The commute data is synthetic — shaped like the real thing and plausibly related to the real scores, but generated. Units 1–3 are unaffected; the capstone's findings are not meaningful until it is replaced. README.md has the three commands.

The shape of it

Unit 1 — Foundations (steps 1–8 · available now)

Read, represent and transform the project's data with nothing but the core language. No packages, no files.

↑ Level up: Jupyter notebooks (available now)

Install Jupyter, learn the one genuinely tricky thing about notebooks — they remember more than they show — and run what you have written against all 632 block groups instead of eleven.

Unit 2 — Correctness (steps 9–12)

Equivalent forms of one computation; reading tracebacks; refactoring while proving the output did not change; the failure modes this data actually has; and real files, which retire the pasted-in sample for good.

Unit 3 — Structure and the ecosystem (steps 13–16)

Modules, classes, and choosing third-party packages — pandas, matplotlib, scipy — then rebuilding the hand-written join to check it against one. Step 16 has you fetch the commute data yourself, which needs a free key from api.census.gov.

↑ Level up: Notebooks to scripts (available now)

Virtual environments, pinned dependencies, and splitting the notebook into fetch / clean / analyze / run. The test is deleting the environment, rebuilding it from a file, and finding out what your analysis secretly depended on.

Unit 4 — Development practice (steps 17–21)

Git, pytest, the reproduce→test→fix→commit cycle against two realistic tickets, project layout, and a capstone that answers the question honestly — margins of error included.
